STANDARD MARINE COMMUNICATION PHRASES
The International Maritime Organization established comprehensive communication standards that create unified language protocols for all maritime radio operations. Standard Marine Communication Phrases provide essential frameworks ensuring precise understanding between vessels, shore stations, and emergency services regardless of crew nationality or language backgrounds.

These standardized phrases replace ambiguous expressions with clear, unambiguous language that prevents dangerous misunderstandings during critical operations. Professional mariners must demonstrate competency in these phrases as required by STCW Convention standards for navigation watch officers aboard vessels over 500 gross tonnage.

Basic Communication Structure
Professional VHF communication follows structured patterns that ensure message clarity while reducing transmission time and preventing channel congestion during busy maritime operations.

Essential communication elements include:
► Station identification: Clear vessel name and call sign identification
► Message markers: Instruction, advice, warning, information, question, answer, request, intention
► Standard responses: Affirmative and negative acknowledgments with full repetition
► Position reporting: Precise location using degrees, minutes, and geographic references
► Time references: UTC notation with clear hour and minute designations

Example (Initial Contact):

Vessel A: "Port Control, Port Control, this is MV Ocean Star, 
call sign Hotel Alpha Bravo Charlie One, on Channel One-Two. 
How do you read me? Over."

Port Control: "MV Ocean Star, this is Port Control. 
Reading you loud and clear. Go ahead. Over."

MV Ocean Star: "Port Control, QUESTION. Can I arrive to berth 
Seven-Alpha 15 minutes later at One-Four-Zero-Zero local time? 
My position bearing One-Four-Zero degrees from Main Light, 
distance Five nautical miles. Over."

Port Control: "MV Ocean Star, ANSWER. Affirmative, you can arrive 
Seven-Alpha at One-Four-Zero-Zero local time. ADVICE. Reduce 
speed to Five knots due to fishing boatd. Over."

MV Ocean Star: "Port Control, I will reduce speed to Five knots. 
Confirm ETA berth Seven-Alpha One-Four-Zero-Zero local time. Over."

Port Control: "MV Ocean Star, confirmed. 
Stand by Channel One-Two. Out."

Spelling and Number Conventions
International phonetic alphabet usage ensures accurate information transmission when spelling becomes necessary during radio communications. Standardized number pronunciation prevents confusion that could result in navigation errors or operational mistakes.

Standard phonetic alphabet includes specific pronunciations:
⌑ Letters: Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, Echo, Foxtrot, Golf, Hotel
⌑ Numbers: Zero (ZEERO), One (WUN), Two (TOO), Three (TREE)
⌑ Special numbers: Four (FOWER), Five (FIFE), Eight (AIT), Nine (NINER)
⌑ Large numbers: Thousand (TOUSAND) with individual digit pronunciation

Position coordinates require careful pronunciation using individual digits for degrees and minutes, followed by cardinal directions. Bearing references use three-digit notation from true north unless specifically stated as relative bearings.

❔ Did you know? Modified number pronunciation reduces confusion between similar-sounding digits during radio transmission, especially in challenging atmospheric conditions.

EMERGENCY COMMUNICATION PROCEDURES
Maritime emergencies demand immediate, clear communication that mobilizes appropriate assistance while providing essential information to rescue coordination centers. Distress communication follows specific protocols that prioritize vessel identification, position reporting, and nature of emergency before requesting specific assistance types.

Emergency communication hierarchy progresses from distress situations requiring immediate assistance through urgency messages involving vessel safety to safety communications warning other vessels about navigation hazards or weather conditions.

Distress Communications
Distress situations involve immediate danger to vessel, crew, or passengers requiring urgent assistance from nearby vessels or shore-based rescue services. MAYDAY calls initiate distress communication sequences that receive priority over all other radio traffic until situations are resolved.

VESSEL TRAFFIC SERVICE COMMUNICATIONS
Vessel Traffic Services provide navigation assistance, traffic organization, and information services that enhance maritime safety in designated areas. VTS communications follow standardized formats ensuring efficient information exchange between vessels and shore-based traffic control centers.

VTS Reporting Requirements
VTS stations require comprehensive vessel information enabling effective traffic management and navigation assistance provision.

Example (VTS Initial Report):

Vessel: "Dover VTS, Dover VTS, this is MV Nordic Star,
call sign Lima One Mike November Eight, on Channel One-Four. Over."

Dover VTS: "MV Nordic Star, this is Dover VTS. Go ahead. Over."

MV Nordic Star: "Dover VTS, this is sailing report.
My position Dover TSS reporting point Alpha.
My course Zero-Nine-Zero degrees, speed Fifteen knots.
Last port Hamburg, destination London.
Draft Twelve point Five meters, air draft Twenty-Eight meters.
Cargo containers, no dangerous goods.
ETA London pilot station Zero-Eight-Three-Zero UTC. Over."

Dover VTS: "MV Nordic Star, roger your sailing report.
INFORMATION. Heavy traffic in separation scheme.
ADVICE. Maintain current course and speed.
Report at next waypoint Bravo. Over."

Example (Pilot Request Sequence):

MT Marine Public: "Pilot Station, Pilot Station, 
this is MT Marine Public, 
call sign Lima One Mike November Eight,  
calling you on Channel One-Four. How do you read? Over."

Reading you loud and clear, go ahead. Over."

MT Marine Public: "Pilot Station, QUESTION. Will Pilot 
board on arrival Pilot Station or I need to stay adrift, 
and pilot boarding arrangements? My position bearing
Two-Seven-Zero degrees, distance Fifteen nautical miles
from pilot station. My freeboard Ten meters. Over."

Pilot Station: "MT Marine Public, ANSWER. 
Pilot boarding on arrival. Pilot boarding speed
Seven knots. Prepare combination ladder One point Five
meters above water, starboard side. Provide good lee side. Over."

MT Marine Public: "Pilot Station, roger. Pilot on arrival, 
Starboard side combination ladder One point Five meters

My ETA pilot station Zero-Seven-Three-Zero UTC. Over."

One-Four and One-Six. Pilot boat call sign Papa Bravo Two. Out."

ON-BOARD COMMUNICATION STANDARDS
Internal vessel communications require standardized procedures ensuring precise understanding between bridge team members, engine room personnel, and deck crews during all operational phases.

Bridge Team Communications
Example (Watch Handover):

Relieving Officer: "Good morning Chief Officer. I am here to relieve you.
What is our present situation?"

Chief Officer: "Good morning Second Officer. Present position
bearing One-Three-Zero degrees, distance Twelve nautical miles
from Cape Light. Course Zero-Nine-Five degrees, speed Fourteen knots.
Next alteration Two points to starboard at waypoint Charlie
in Forty-Five minutes."

Relieving Officer: "Roger. What is the traffic situation?"

Chief Officer: "Container vessel overtaking on port side,
CPA One nautical mile in Twenty minutes. Fishing boats
in area around waypoint Charlie. Radar set to Six mile range,
GPS operational, autopilot engaged."

Relieving Officer: "Understood. Any standing orders?"

Chief Officer: "Call Master if visibility drops below Two nautical miles
or any vessel closes to less than One nautical mile CPA.
Reduce speed if necessary for safe passage through fishing fleet."

Relieving Officer: "I understand the situation and accept the watch."

Chief Officer: "You have the watch."

Example (Engine Orders):

GOOD TO KNOW
► Standard Marine Communication Phrases are mandatory for officers in charge of navigation watch on vessels over 500 gross tonnage
► Message markers must precede communications to clarify intent: instruction, advice, warning, information, question, answer, request, intention
► Phonetic alphabet uses specific pronunciations like ZEERO for zero, WUN for one, and FOWER for four to prevent transmission errors
► Position reporting requires degrees and minutes with cardinal directions, avoiding decimal degrees for radio communication
► Time references must use UTC notation clearly stating hours and minutes to prevent timezone confusion
► Emergency communication hierarchy progresses from MAYDAY (distress) through PAN-PAN (urgency) to SECURITE (safety)
► VTS communications require accurate vessel information including dimensions, cargo details, and operational restrictions
► Pilot communications address boarding arrangements, safety equipment, and weather conditions before transfer operations
► Weather reporting follows meteorological standards using cardinal points for direction and Beaufort scale for wind force
► Bridge team communication requires systematic information sharing with clear command acknowledgment procedures
