What Is a Marine VHF Radio?
A marine VHF radio is a two-way communication device designed specifically for watercraft. VHF stands for Very High Frequency, and these radios operate between 156 and 162 megahertz on designated marine channels. Unlike cell phones that require expensive infrastructure and monthly subscriptions, VHF radios use free public airwaves that work instantly with the push of a button.

How VHF Communication Works?
Marine VHF radios use simplex communication, meaning transmissions travel one direction at a time. When you press the Push-to-Talk button, you transmit. When you release it, you receive. This is different from phone conversations where both parties speak simultaneously. The radio waves travel in a straight line, giving them a range of roughly 20 miles depending on antenna height and conditions.

The higher your antenna, the farther your signal travels. A fixed-mount radio with an antenna mounted 15 feet above the waterline can typically reach 15 to 20 miles to another vessel or shore station. Handheld units with their antennas just a few feet above water level usually work reliably within 3 to 5 miles of shore.

Fixed-Mount vs Handheld VHF Radios
Fixed-mount radios install permanently on your boat and connect to an external antenna. They typically transmit at 25 watts, giving them maximum range. Most run off your boat’s 12-volt electrical system with battery backup. These are the standard for most recreational vessels because of their superior range and reliability.

Handheld VHF radios are portable units that work anywhere. They usually transmit at 1 to 6 watts, limiting their range significantly. The big advantage is redundancy. If your boat loses power, a handheld still works. Many experienced boaters carry both types, using the fixed mount as primary and the handheld as backup or for dinghy trips.

How to Use a VHF Marine Radio for Beginners?
Operating a VHF radio is simpler than most people expect. Once you understand the basic steps, you can communicate confidently with other boaters and shore stations. Here is the five-step process that will have you using your radio like a pro.

Step 1: Select the Correct Channel
Turn on your radio and select the appropriate channel for your communication. For hailing another vessel or making initial contact, use Channel 16. For bridge communications, switch to Channel 13. If you are calling a marina or need a weather update, check the channel guide usually posted near your radio or in your owner’s manual. The channel selector is typically a knob or buttons on the front panel.

Step 2: Adjust the Squelch Control
The squelch control eliminates static and background noise when no one is transmitting. Turn the squelch knob until the static noise just disappears. If you set it too low, you will hear constant static. If you set it too high, you might miss weak transmissions from distant stations. Find the sweet spot where the speaker stays quiet until someone actually speaks.

Step 3: Push the PTT Button
PTT stands for Push-to-Talk. Hold down this button on your microphone or radio body while speaking. The radio transmits only while you hold this button down. Many radios have a transmit indicator light that glows red when you are broadcasting. Wait a half-second after pressing PTT before speaking to ensure the radio fully activates.

Step 4: Speak Normally at Close Range
Hold the microphone about two inches from your mouth and speak in a normal conversational tone. Do not shout. Modern VHF radios compress and transmit your voice clearly at normal volume. Shouting actually distorts your transmission. Speak clearly and at a moderate pace. Identify yourself using your boat name or registration number.

Step 5: Release to Listen
Release the PTT button immediately after finishing your message. Remember, you cannot receive while transmitting. If you hold the button too long, you might miss a response or block others from using the channel. After releasing, listen for a response. If you do not hear anything within 15 seconds, you can try again.

Understanding Channel 16 on Marine Radio
Channel 16 is the most important frequency on your marine VHF radio. Operating at 156.800 megahertz, it serves as the international distress, safety, and calling channel. Every marine VHF radio in North America and most of the world monitors this frequency as the default channel when powered on.

What Is Channel 16 Used For?
Channel 16 serves three distinct purposes. First, it is the emergency channel for MAYDAY calls when life or property is in immediate danger. Second, it functions as the universal hailing channel for making initial contact with other vessels, marinas, or shore stations. Third, the Coast Guard and other rescue authorities monitor Channel 16 continuously to respond to distress calls.

Think of Channel 16 as a busy emergency room entrance. You go there to get attention, but you move to a private room for actual conversation. Use Channel 16 to hail another station, then immediately switch to a working channel for extended communication. This keeps Channel 16 available for emergencies.

Why the Coast Guard Monitors Channel 16
The United States Coast Guard maintains continuous radio watch on Channel 16 from shore stations, cutters, and aircraft. This monitoring network provides a safety net for boaters in distress. When you make a MAYDAY call on Channel 16, you are not just calling for help from nearby boats. You are alerting professional rescue services who can coordinate a response.

Beyond distress calls, Coast Guard broadcasts important safety information on Channel 16, including navigation warnings, weather alerts, and notices to mariners. Staying tuned to Channel 16 while underway keeps you informed about hazards and changing conditions in your area.

Essential Marine Radio Channels Every Boater Needs
Beyond Channel 16, several other channels serve specific purposes that every boater should understand. Knowing which channel to use for different situations makes your communications more efficient and helps keep emergency channels clear.

Channel 9: Boater Calling Channel
Channel 9 serves as an alternative hailing channel for recreational boaters. Many areas encourage using Channel 9 instead of Channel 16 for routine hailing to keep Channel 16 available for distress calls. While not universally adopted, Channel 9 is particularly useful in busy boating areas where Channel 16 experiences heavy traffic.

Channel 13: Bridge-to-Bridge Navigation
Channel 13 is designated for bridge-to-bridge communications between vessels and with shore stations regarding navigation safety. Use this channel to contact drawbridges for opening requests, communicate with locks, and coordinate passing arrangements with commercial traffic. All vessels over 20 meters must monitor Channel 13 while navigating.

Channel 22A: Coast Guard Liaison
After establishing contact with the Coast Guard on Channel 16, they will often ask you to switch to Channel 22A for continued communication. This channel serves as the working frequency for Coast Guard operations. Some marine radios have a dedicated button to quickly switch to Channel 22A. Remember to switch back to Channel 16 after your Coast Guard communication ends.

Weather Channels (WX)
Modern VHF radios include dedicated weather channels labeled WX1 through WX7. These receive continuous broadcasts from NOAA Weather Radio with current conditions, forecasts, and emergency alerts. The specific frequency varies by region, but your radio should automatically tune to the strongest weather signal available. Monitoring weather channels before and during trips helps you avoid dangerous conditions.

Working Channels for Routine Communication
Once you establish contact on Channel 16 or 9, you need a working channel for actual conversation. Channels 68, 69, 71, 72, and 78A serve as non-commercial working channels for recreational boaters. When hailing another vessel, you might suggest one of these channels. Some areas have local conventions about which working channels to use, so listening to local traffic helps you follow regional customs.

How to Hail Another Vessel or Marina in 2026?
Hailing is the process of making initial contact with another station. Doing it properly shows you understand radio etiquette and gets you a faster response. Many new boaters feel nervous about their first hails, but following a simple script makes the process straightforward.

The Standard Hailing Protocol
Start by tuning to Channel 16. Listen briefly to ensure the channel is not in use. Then press your PTT button and say the name of the station you are calling, followed by your own vessel name, then the word “over.” For example: “Marina Del Ray, Marina Del Ray, this is sailing vessel Windward, over.”

Wait 15 to 30 seconds for a response. If you do not hear anything, try again. If there is still no response after three attempts, the station may not be monitoring or may be out of range. Once the station responds, they will either answer your question immediately or suggest switching to a working channel for the conversation.

Sample Hailing Script
Here is a typical hailing exchange between two recreational vessels:

Calling vessel: “Sea Breeze, Sea Breeze, this is Blue Horizon on Channel 16, over.”

Called vessel: “Blue Horizon, this is Sea Breeze, go ahead, over.”

Calling vessel: “Sea Breeze, let’s switch to Channel 68 for a working channel, over.”

Called vessel: “Blue Horizon, switching to 68, out.”

Both vessels then switch their radios to Channel 68 and continue their conversation without tying up Channel 16.

Ending a Radio Conversation
Properly ending a conversation frees up the channel for others. Use “over” when you expect a response from the other station. Use “out” when the conversation is completely finished. You can combine them as “over and out” to indicate you have nothing more to say and expect no further response.

Never say “over and out” if you are waiting for the other station to respond. This confuses them. Say “over” when you want them to speak next. Say “out” when the conversation is truly finished. On working channels, a simple “Blue Horizon out” is sufficient to indicate you are done.

Emergency Procedures: MAYDAY, Pan-Pan, and Securite
Knowing how to call for help properly can save lives. Marine radios have three distinct levels of urgency for different situations. Using the wrong level either wastes resources or delays critical response. Understanding these distinctions keeps you and other boaters safe.

MAYDAY: Life-Threatening Emergency
MAYDAY signals grave and imminent danger requiring immediate assistance. Use MAYDAY when lives are threatened, a vessel is sinking, someone has a medical emergency, or fire is out of control. MAYDAY calls get absolute priority on the radio.

To make a MAYDAY call, tune to Channel 16, set your radio to high power, and transmit: “MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY. This is [vessel name], [vessel name], [vessel name].” Then provide your MMSI number if you have one, your position using latitude and longitude or bearing and distance from a known point, the nature of your distress, the kind of assistance you need, and the number of people on board.

Continue calling MAYDAY until you get a response. If you have a DSC-equipped radio, press the distress button to automatically alert the Coast Guard with your position and vessel information.

Pan-Pan: Urgent But Not Life-Threatening
Pan-Pan (pronounced pahn-pahn) indicates an urgent situation that does not pose immediate danger to life. Use Pan-Pan when you have a mechanical breakdown, medical issue that is not immediately critical, or are drifting toward danger but have time to resolve the situation.

The procedure is similar to MAYDAY but with lower priority. Transmit: “Pan-Pan, Pan-Pan, Pan-Pan. This is [vessel name], [vessel name].” Then provide your position, the nature of your urgent situation, and what assistance you need. The Coast Guard and other stations will respond, but MAYDAY calls take precedence if multiple emergencies occur.

Securite: Safety Information Broadcast
Securite (pronounced say-cure-eh-tay) announces important safety information that is not an emergency. Use this to warn other boaters about hazards you have spotted, such as floating debris, a partially submerged object, or a disabled vessel in a channel. Coast Guard also uses Securite for navigation warnings and severe weather alerts.

Format your Securite call as: “Securite, Securite, Securite. All stations, all stations, this is [vessel name].” Then describe the hazard, your position, and any recommended action. Other boaters will thank you for the heads-up.

Warning About False Distress Calls
False MAYDAY calls are federal crimes punishable by heavy fines and imprisonment. Never make a MAYDAY call as a joke or for practice. The Coast Guard responds to every MAYDAY with full rescue resources, which costs tens of thousands of dollars per deployment and potentially delays response to real emergencies.

To practice radio procedures, use Channel 9 for radio checks or call a friend on a working channel. If you accidentally transmit a distress call, immediately transmit a cancellation: “This is [vessel name], disregard my previous transmission, no distress, I say again, no distress.” Then monitor Channel 16 and explain the mistake to the Coast Guard if they respond.

VHF Radio Etiquette and Best Practices
Following proper radio etiquette makes you a responsible member of the boating community. Good etiquette keeps emergency channels clear, ensures your communications get through, and shows respect for fellow boaters. These unwritten rules separate experienced operators from beginners.

Keep Channel 16 Clear
Channel 16 is for hailing and emergencies only. Never use it for routine conversation, radio checks, or casual chatter. After making contact on Channel 16, switch to a working channel immediately. If someone calls you on Channel 16, respond briefly and suggest a working channel for the actual conversation. Extended discussions on Channel 16 block emergency calls and annoy other boaters.

Monitor While Underway
Keep your radio tuned to Channel 16 whenever your engine is running. This lets you hear distress calls from other vessels that might need your help. You will also receive important Coast Guard safety broadcasts. Even if you do not expect to make calls yourself, listening keeps you informed about local traffic, weather warnings, and potential hazards.

Use Proper Power Settings
Most fixed-mount radios have high and low power settings. Use high power (25 watts) for long-range communication, hailing distant stations, or emergency calls. Use low power (1 watt) when talking to nearby vessels to reduce interference with other stations. Low power also saves electricity and reduces wear on your radio. When in doubt, start on high power for hailing, then reduce power once contact is established if the other station is nearby.

Prohibited Communications
Federal regulations prohibit several types of radio communications. Never transmit profanity, false distress calls, or general broadcasts to all stations except for Securite safety messages. Do not transmit music or entertainment programming. Commercial advertising is forbidden. Personal business messages not related to vessel operations are prohibited. Violations can result in fines and license suspension.

Common Etiquette Mistakes to Avoid
New boaters often make these etiquette errors. Avoid saying “over and out” together when you expect a response. Do not speak without identifying your vessel first. Never hold the PTT button while thinking about what to say. Do not interrupt another conversation unless you have an emergency. Avoid using CB radio slang like “breaker breaker” or “10-4” which is not used on marine frequencies. Remember that all radio transmissions are public and recorded, so keep your language professional.

DSC, MMSI, and Modern VHF Features (2026)
Modern VHF radios include Digital Selective Calling, a technology that transforms marine safety. DSC radios can send distress alerts with the press of one button, automatically including your vessel information and GPS position. Understanding these features adds a powerful safety layer to your boating.

What Is Digital Selective Calling?
DSC adds digital messaging capability to standard voice VHF radios. Instead of broadcasting to everyone on a channel, DSC lets you send digital messages to specific vessels or shore stations. The most important DSC feature is the distress button, typically protected by a flip-cover to prevent accidental activation.

When you press the DSC distress button, your radio automatically sends an alert on Channel 70 (the dedicated DSC data channel) containing your MMSI number, vessel information, and GPS coordinates if connected. The Coast Guard receives this alert within seconds and can respond immediately knowing exactly where you are.

Getting Your MMSI Number
MMSI stands for Maritime Mobile Service Identity. This nine-digit number uniquely identifies your vessel in the DSC system. You need an MMSI number to use DSC features on your radio. For recreational boaters, obtaining an MMSI is free and takes just minutes online.

BoatUS, Sea Tow, and the FCC all issue MMSI numbers for US recreational vessels. Visit the BoatUS MMSI registration page or Sea Tow’s website to complete the simple application. You will receive your number immediately, which you then program into your radio following the manufacturer’s instructions. Write your MMSI number down and keep it accessible in case you need to provide it during an emergency voice call.

GPS Integration Importance
If your radio connects to a GPS receiver (built-in or external), your DSC distress calls automatically include your precise location. Without GPS integration, you must verbally provide your position during emergencies, which takes time and may be inaccurate if you are stressed. GPS-connected radios also let you send your position to other DSC-equipped vessels and receive their positions, showing them on chartplotters as AIS-like targets.

Practice Exercises for Beginners
Confidence comes from practice. These exercises help you build radio skills in low-pressure situations before you need them in an emergency. Work through them in order, and you will feel comfortable using your VHF radio in any situation.

Exercise 1: Radio Check Procedure
Start by testing your radio without causing false alarms. Tune to Channel 9 and call for a radio check: “This is [vessel name], requesting a radio check on Channel 9, over.” If another boater hears you, they will respond confirming your transmission quality. Some areas have automated radio check systems that respond with a recorded message. Listen for responses and note how clearly your transmission comes through. Practice this at your dock or anchorage before you actually need to rely on the radio offshore.

Exercise 2: Marina Hailing Practice
Plan a short trip to a marina you have never visited. Before departing, look up the marina’s name and VHF channel if available. When you are about two miles out, practice hailing them using the proper protocol: “[Marina name], [Marina name], this is [your vessel name] inbound, over.” Go through the entire exchange including switching to a working channel. Even if you already know the answer to your question, practicing the procedure builds muscle memory.

Exercise 3: Listening to Channel 16
Spend an afternoon listening to Channel 16 from your dock or while anchored near a busy waterway. Do not transmit, just listen. You will hear the rhythm of professional communications, learn the local channels used in your area, and understand how quickly working channel switches happen. Note the types of calls that come through and how experienced boaters phrase their messages. This passive learning prepares you for active participation.

Exercise 4: Phonetic Alphabet Practice
Practice spelling your vessel name, registration numbers, and your own name using the phonetic alphabet. Do this until it becomes automatic. Try spelling random words you see around you using phonetic alphabet words. The goal is eliminating hesitation when you need to spell something clearly on the radio. Record yourself and listen back to ensure you are pronouncing each word clearly.

Troubleshooting Common VHF Problems
Even well-maintained radios sometimes develop problems. Knowing how to diagnose and fix common issues keeps you communicating when it matters. These are the most frequent problems boaters encounter and their solutions.

No Sound Even When Radio Is On
If your radio powers up but you hear nothing, first check the volume control. Then adjust the squelch knob counterclockwise until you hear static. If static appears but no voices, the radio is working but no one is transmitting nearby. If you hear nothing at all even with squelch fully counterclockwise, check your external speaker connections if applicable. Handheld radios should produce sound from their built-in speakers. If completely silent, the speaker may be damaged or the audio circuit has failed.

Cannot Transmit (Transmit Light Does Not Come On)
When you press PTT, a transmit indicator (usually red) should glow. If nothing happens, verify you are on a transmit-capable channel. Some channels like WX weather and Channel 70 (DSC data) are receive-only. Switch to Channel 16 or another working channel. Check that your microphone is properly connected. For handhelds, verify the battery has sufficient charge. If using an external microphone, try the radio’s built-in PTT button if equipped. If still no transmit, the microphone or PTT circuit may need repair.

Static and Interference Issues
Excessive static usually indicates improper squelch adjustment. Turn the squelch knob clockwise until the static just disappears. If static persists or you hear breaking voices, you may be receiving distant stations weakly. This is normal for VHF. If you hear engine noise through the radio, your power connections may need filtering. Ignition noise creates a specific ticking sound that increases with engine RPM. Installing noise filters on power leads often solves this.

Range Problems
If other stations report your signal is weak or you cannot reach stations you previously heard, check your antenna connections. Corroded connections at the antenna base are the most common range killers. Inspect the antenna for physical damage. Antennas do degrade over time, typically lasting 5 to 10 years depending on exposure to sun and salt. If using a handheld, remember that 1 watt of power has inherently limited range. For offshore work, you need a 25-watt fixed mount with a quality external antenna mounted as high as practical.

Testing Your Radio Properly
Never test your radio by calling MAYDAY or transmitting on Channel 16 unnecessarily. Use Channel 9 for radio checks. In some areas, automated radio check systems exist on Channel 9, 24, 27, or 28 depending on your location. These systems record your transmission and play it back so you hear how you sound. Alternatively, arrange with a boating friend to test radios on a working channel. The Coast Guard also permits brief radio checks on Channel 16 during periods of low activity, but use this sparingly and keep it brief.
