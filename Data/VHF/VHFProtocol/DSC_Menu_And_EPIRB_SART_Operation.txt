DSC RADIO MENU OPERATION AND EPIRB/SART HANDLING

The examples below describe how to physically operate a DSC (Digital Selective Calling) VHF radio's menu system, and how to correctly stow, read, and test an EPIRB and a SART. These are standardized, ITU-mandated concepts (ITU-R M.493 defines the DSC call categories) common to virtually every marine DSC radio, even though the exact button layout varies slightly between manufacturers. Knowing the menu structure and the safety-equipment handling procedure is as important as knowing what to say over the microphone.

DSC Menu Navigation Basics

Most DSC sets are entered by pressing a dedicated MENU or CALL/MENU key. From the top-level menu, the up/down channel keys or a rotary knob scroll through the main categories, which are typically DISTRESS, INDIVIDUAL CALL, GROUP CALL, ALL SHIPS, POSITION REQUEST/REPORT, and LOG. Pressing ENTER (or the equivalent OK key) on a highlighted category opens it; pressing the MENU/CALL key again, or a dedicated BACK/CLEAR key, exits back up one level.

To find a stored contact from the menu, scroll to INDIVIDUAL CALL, press ENTER, then scroll through the stored directory of MMSI numbers by vessel name rather than typing the full MMSI digit by digit.

Making A DSC Distress Call

A DSC distress alert is sent by lifting (or flipping open) the dedicated DISTRESS button cover and pressing and holding the DISTRESS button for approximately five seconds, until the radio confirms the alert has been sent (usually a beep and a change of display). If time and safety allow, the operator should also select the nature of distress (fire, flooding, collision, and so on) from the short menu presented before the five-second hold completes; if there is no time, an undesignated distress alert with position and time is still sent and can be corrected later. After sending the DSC distress alert, the operator should immediately follow up with a voice MAYDAY call on Channel 16.

Making An Individual DSC Call

To call one specific vessel by DSC: press MENU, scroll to INDIVIDUAL CALL, press ENTER, select or enter the target vessel's MMSI, select the working channel to propose for the follow-up voice conversation, then press CALL or ENTER to transmit. The called vessel's radio will sound an alert and display the calling MMSI; accepting the call typically auto-tunes both radios to the proposed working channel.

Making A Group DSC Call

A GROUP CALL alerts every radio programmed with a shared group MMSI (for example, a fishing fleet or a group of vessels in company) rather than one single vessel. From the menu, scroll to GROUP CALL, select the stored group MMSI, choose a working channel, and transmit; every set in that group will alert and can then switch to the proposed channel together.

Making An All Ships DSC Call

An ALL SHIPS category is used for safety/urgency broadcasts rather than routine traffic, and does not require entering any MMSI, since it addresses every DSC-equipped vessel in range. From the menu, select ALL SHIPS, choose the appropriate category (for example SAFETY), and transmit; this is typically followed by the actual safety voice broadcast on the working channel indicated.

Sending A DSC Position Request Or Report

To ask another station for its position: select POSITION REQUEST from the menu, enter or select their MMSI, and transmit; a compatible station will normally auto-reply with its current position. To send your own position proactively, select POSITION REPORT/MSI, confirm the position source (GPS-fed automatically, or manually entered), and transmit.

Cancelling A Routine DSC Call Before It Is Transmitted

During the compose or preview step, before pressing CALL/SEND, press ESC, CANCEL, or CLEAR to abort the call entirely and return to the main menu with nothing transmitted. If SEND has already been pressed but the set is still in its short pre-transmission delay, pressing CANCEL again will still block transmission on many models; once the call has actually gone out over the air, it cannot be recalled and a correction must be sent as a new call.

Muting And Un-Muting DSC Alarms

When an incoming DSC alert sounds, pressing the ALARM CANCEL or SILENCE key mutes the audible tone for that specific alert while keeping the set's DSC watch-keeping active on Channel 70. The operator should then read the alert details on the display, take the appropriate action (answer, switch channel, log it), and confirm afterward that the alarm system itself is still fully armed and not left permanently silenced.

Manually Entering Position If GPS Input Fails

If the radio loses its automatic GPS position feed, the operator can usually select MANUAL POSITION or POSITION ENTRY from the DSC/position menu, then key in latitude (degrees, minutes, hemisphere N/S) and longitude (degrees, minutes, hemisphere E/W) along with the UTC time of that position, so that any DSC distress alert sent afterward still carries a usable position rather than none at all. Manually entered positions should be updated regularly, since the set will not update them automatically the way it does with a live GPS feed.

EPIRB Storage And Readiness

An EPIRB (Emergency Position Indicating Radio Beacon) should be stowed in its dedicated bracket in an easily reachable location, typically near a companionway, helm station, or abandon-ship point, with the Hydrostatic Release Unit (HRU) armed so the beacon can float free and activate automatically if the vessel sinks. The bracket and HRU should be visually checked periodically for corrosion or expiry, since a seized bracket or an expired HRU can prevent automatic release in a real emergency.

SART Storage And Readiness

A SART (Search And Rescue Transponder) or AIS-SART should be stowed close to the liferaft or the grab bag, not buried in a locker, so it can be grabbed in the same motion as abandoning ship. It should be kept switched off until needed, tested only per the manufacturer's specified test procedure, and mounted as high as practical once activated, since its detection range depends heavily on antenna height above the water.

Reading And Interpreting An EPIRB Label

The label on an EPIRB carries several distinct pieces of information that should be read in a specific order: the unique fifteen-character hexadecimal (HEX) identity code used by the Cospas-Sarsat satellite system to identify the specific beacon and its registered owner; the MMSI, if the beacon has been programmed with one; the battery expiry date, after which the transmitter is no longer guaranteed to operate for the full rated duration; the Hydrostatic Release Unit expiry date, which is a separate, shorter-lived component; and the manufacturer, model, and country-code registration reference, which are needed if the beacon must be re-registered or serviced.

Testing An EPIRB Without Triggering A Real Alert

Nearly all EPIRBs have a dedicated TEST position or a short press-and-release test sequence that transmits a brief, low-power self-test burst rather than a full distress signal; this should only be done during the manufacturer's specified test window (for example the first few minutes of the hour) so that any monitoring station recognizes it as a routine test rather than a real activation. The operator should never hold the activation switch in the full ON/ACTIVATE position to "test" the unit, since that sends a genuine distress alert to the search-and-rescue system.

Registering And Re-Registering Safety Beacons

An EPIRB and, where applicable, a SART's MMSI-linked registration must be kept current with the vessel's actual details (owner contact numbers, vessel description, next of kin) at the relevant national registration authority, since search-and-rescue services rely on this registration record, not the beacon itself, to know who to look for and who to contact first when an alert is received.
